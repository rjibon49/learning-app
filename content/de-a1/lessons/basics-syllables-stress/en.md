Long German words look scary, but they are built from short pieces called **Silben** (syllables). Read them piece by piece.

## What is a syllable?

A syllable is one beat of a word. Every syllable has one vowel sound.

| Deutsch | Syllables | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Tag | Tag | 1 |
| Mama | Ma-ma | 2 |
| Banane | Ba-na-ne | 3 |
| Telefon | Te-le-fon | 3 |
| Wochenende | Wo-chen-en-de | 4 |
| Kindergarten | Kin-der-gar-ten | 4 |

## Long words are just short words glued together

German joins words to make new ones. Split them and you understand them:

| Deutsch | Parts | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Hausaufgabe | Haus + Aufgabe | homework (house + task) |
| Kühlschrank | kühl + Schrank | fridge (cool + cupboard) |
| Handschuh | Hand + Schuh | glove (hand + shoe) |
| Bahnhof | Bahn + Hof | train station |

The **last** word decides the article: die Hausaufgabe (because **die** Aufgabe), der Handschuh (because **der** Schuh).

## Which syllable is stressed?

- Most German words stress the **first** syllable: **Va**-ter, **Mut**-ter, **Ta**-sche
- Words with **be-, ge-, ver-, er-** stress the next syllable: be-**su**-chen, ver-**ste**-hen
- Many foreign words stress the end: Ca-**fé**, Stu-**dent**, Mu-**sik**, Te-le-**fon**

> **Tip:** Clap the syllables while you speak — one clap per vowel sound. It works for every German word.
