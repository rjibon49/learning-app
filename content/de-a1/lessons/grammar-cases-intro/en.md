In German the **article changes** depending on the job a noun does in the sentence. These jobs are called **cases** (Fälle). At A1 you need three of them.

## The three A1 cases

| Case | Question | Job in the sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Nominativ | Wer? Was? | the subject — who does it |
| Akkusativ | Wen? Was? | the direct object — what/whom it is done to |
| Dativ | Wem? | the receiver — to/for whom |

## One sentence, three cases

| Deutsch | Case |
|---|---|
| Der Mann | Nominativ — who gives? |
| gibt | verb |
| dem Kind | Dativ — to whom? |
| einen Apfel. | Akkusativ — what? |

"Der Mann gibt dem Kind einen Apfel." = The man gives the child an apple.

## Why it matters

Because the article shows the job, German word order can change and the meaning stays clear:

- "Der Hund sieht den Mann." — The dog sees the man.
- "Den Mann sieht der Hund." — The dog sees the man (focus on the man).

**der** = subject, **den** = object. The article tells you, not the position.

## The full table (you will learn it step by step)

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominativ | der / ein | die / eine | das / ein | die / – |
| Akkusativ | den / einen | die / eine | das / ein | die / – |
| Dativ | dem / einem | der / einer | dem / einem | den / – (+n) |

> **Good news:** feminine, neuter and plural look the same in Nominativ and Akkusativ. Only **masculine** changes (der → den).
